                                                                                               Abstract
                                                   We study stochastic fixed-point iterations xn+1 = θn x0 + (1 − θn )Tb(xn ) for nonexpansive
                                                   and contractive operators on Hilbert spaces, with a single-point unbiased oracle of bounded
                                                   variance. Deterministically, every anchor schedule θn = cn /(n + 2) whose density cn ∈ (0, 1]
                                                   does not oscillate between scales is either polynomially suboptimal on contractions or super-
                                                   polynomially slow on rotations; for constant densities θn = c/(n + 2) the tradeoff is exact,
                                                   with ∥xn ∥ ≍ Γ(c + 1)(nφ)−c on rotations by angle φ (φ → 0, nφ → ∞) and, for the classical
                                                   schedule, ∥xn ∥ = 2| sin(nφ/2)|
                                                                             nφ      + O( n1 ). The dichotomy fails exactly under lacunary anchor
                                                   concentration: an explicit γ-oblivious schedule, one per target accuracy, is simultaneously
                                                   contraction-optimal and rotation-polynomial with exponent 1/α0 , α0 = log2 (3/2), and, for each
                                                   fixed target accuracy, the matching lower bound holds for Lebesgue-a.e. angle. For stochastic
                                                   contractions, the minimax rate is Θ(σ 2 ε−2 (1 − γ)−2 ) on affine maps, and within the anchored
                                                   class Θ(σ 2 ε−2 (1 − γ)−2 + (1 − γ)−1 ln(D/ε)) for known modulus via geometric batching; without
                                                   a certified modulus bound no sound distance certificate exists, and a certified ceiling is the exact
                                                   boundary. For nonexpansive maps we prove O(Kσ 2 D2 ε−4 ) in every 2-uniformly smooth Banach
                                                   space and the Hilbert rate Θ̃(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 ) by reduction to monotone inclusions. Numerical
                                                   experiments are consistent with every scaling prediction.

                                           1       Introduction
                                           Let H be a real Hilbert space. A map T : H → H is nonexpansive if ∥T x − T y∥ ≤ ∥x − y∥, and a
                                           γ-contraction if ∥T x − T y∥ ≤ γ∥x − y∥ with γ ∈ (0, 1). We study the anchored iteration

                                                                                 xn+1 = θn x0 + (1 − θn ) Tb(xn ),                                        (1)

                                           where x0 is an anchor, θn ∈ [0, 1] is an anchor schedule, and Tb is a stochastic oracle: each evaluation
                                           of Tb(x) returns T (x) + ξ with E[ξ] = 0 and E∥ξ∥2 ≤ σ 2 , evaluations are independent across calls.
                                           Rather than evaluating Tb once per step, the algorithm may average a batch of mn evaluations at
                                           xn , reducing the per-step variance to σ 2 /mn at mn times the oracle cost. The oracle complexity of
                                           an algorithm on an instance (T, x0 ) is the total number of oracle evaluations required to produce a
                                           point x̂ with E∥x̂ − T x̂∥ ≤ ε (or E∥x̂ − x∗ ∥ ≤ ε for contractions, where x∗ is the unique fixed point),
                                           and we ask for the minimax complexity over instance classes.

rates for the second, and reduce the third to the monotone-inclusion rate in Hilbert space.

1.1   The three questions and our answers
Q1. When is an adaptive anchor schedule necessary? Deterministically (exact oracle), the
classical Halpern schedule θn = 1/(n + 2) is worst-case optimal for nonexpansive maps [24, 13], and
anchored schemes exploiting known contractivity achieve the optimal geometric rate on contractions
[29]; fully parameter-free deterministic variants have appeared very recently [28]. But for a
fixed, γ-oblivious schedule it was unclear whether any separation holds: perhaps one schedule
is simultaneously optimal on both classes. We show the opposite, for the family of schedules
θn = c/(n + 2) with fixed local anchor mass c (containing the classical schedule θn = 1/(n + 2) as
c = 1):

 • (Contraction side, Theorem 3.1) if c ≤ ε/(96D), the schedule reaches ε on every γ-contraction in
   Θ(ln(1/ε)/(1 − γ)) iterations — matching the γ-aware optimum [29];

a factor 2 in evaluations. We also show that the natural single-trajectory escape (Theorem 3.30),
adaptive window restarts, improves the classical contraction time only by a constant factor —
O(D/(εδ)), not the optimum O(ln(D/ε)/δ) — so the separation barrier is robust against this entire
adaptive class, and the parallel scheme is currently the only proved route to two-regime optima.
Finally we prove that the naive route to single-trajectory optimality — detecting contractivity
from trajectory statistics — is obstructed (Remark 3.32): the hidden-direction construction of
Theorem 5.19 works even with exact oracle responses, so single-trajectory optimality, if achievable,
must be structural (residual-feedback) as in [28, 15]. Together with the oscillatory-case theorem
(Theorem 3.33), this gives a complete dichotomy for fixed versus adaptive anchoring, up to the
pointwise lacunarity law and the constant-factor overhead question. The matching lower bound
for the oscillatory escape’s rotation exponent holds, at each fixed accuracy, for Lebesgue-a.e. angle
(Theorem 3.21(i)); the pointwise statement survives only for Diophantine angles (Conjecture 3.22).

What one cannot do, in general, is certify the distance without modulus information: the residual only

The two regimes above are the positive cases.

Q3. What does anchoring cost in the general nonexpansive regime? For nonexpansive
maps the best general bound was Õ(σ 2 ε−5 ) in normed spaces [8], and the Hilbert exponent was open:
the known Ω(σ 2 ε−3 ) lower bound [8] is proved in ℓ1 -type spaces. We address the question in three
steps. First we prove an elementary O(σ 2 D2 ε−4 ) bound in expectation for general nonexpansive
maps (Theorem 4.3), with a single-point oracle and no monotonicity assumptions, via a master
second-moment lemma (Lemma 4.1) that also generates the contraction-regime noise floor, unifying
Q2 and Q3. The proof of Theorem 4.3 uses the inner product once, in the expansion of the squared
deviation norm; the correct Banach-space substitute is 2-uniform smoothness (Remark 4.4): the
duality-mapping inequality recovers the master recursion with the smoothness constant K in place
of 1, and the bound — which uses only nonexpansiveness, the 2-Lipschitzness of x 7→ x − T x, and
the sharp Halpern residual bound of [24, 13] — extends to every 2-uniformly smooth Banach space,
in particular ℓp , Lp for 2 ≤ p < ∞, improving the previously available bound from Õ(σ 2 ε−5 ) to
O(Kσ 2 D2 ε−4 ) on those spaces; a subsequent high-probability bound of Õ(Dσ 2 ε−3 + Dε−1 ) — linear
in the diameter, up to polylogarithmic factors — on weak-type-2 spaces was proved in [17] (see
Section 7). The scope is sharp: the centered recursion itself fails on ℓp , 1 < p < 2 (Remark 4.4), and
the lower-bound Ω(σ 2 ε−3 ) instances of [8] are ℓ1 -type, which are not 2-uniformly smooth either; the
exponent in genuinely arbitrary normed spaces remains open. Second, we observe (Theorem 4.8)
that F = I − T is monotone and 2-Lipschitz for every nonexpansive T on a Hilbert space, so the
residual problem is a monotone inclusion with a single-point oracle; the near-optimal algorithm
of Chen and Luo [11] then gives Õ(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 ), and their lower bound (whose hard instances
with L = 2 embed into nonexpansive maps T = I − ∇f ) gives Ω(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 ) up to logarithms.
The Hilbert-space minimax rate is therefore Θ̃(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 ): the exponent is 2, not 3 or 4.
We emphasize that Theorem 4.8 is a reduction to [11], not a new algorithm; the self-contained
contribution on Q3 is Theorem 4.3 with its general-normed-space validity.

1.2    Techniques and the measurability discipline
The central technical difficulty in stochastic analyses of (1) with adaptive or estimated parameters
is correlation: when θn depends on past oracle responses, it is Fn -measurable, correlated with the
current noise, and martingale orthogonality arguments applied after a triangle-inequality expansion
fail. Our analyses avoid this pitfall structurally. We use: (i) squared-norm recursions expanded
before any norm inequality, so cross terms vanish by conditional unbiasedness of fresh batches; (ii)
deterministic schedules, so that no independence between parameters and noise is ever assumed
— every algorithm in this paper uses either deterministic coefficients or coefficients copied from


                                                  4
an independent deterministic comparison path; (iii) comparison sequences whose parameters are
copied exactly. All stochastic bounds in this paper are derived from a single master noise lemma
(Lemma 4.1). This is also where certification schemes that estimate moduli from the same trajectory
fail (Remark 5.13); it explains why our upper bounds assume deterministic coefficients.

1.3   Related work
Deterministic fixed-point iteration. The Halpern iteration [19] and its convergence theory
[37, 38] are classical; the optimal O(1/n) residual was proved in [24] and shown tight with explicit
constants in normed spaces in [13]; exact optimal complexity for anchored schemes, with matching
lower bounds, is established in [29]. Anchored two-step schemes and modified Mann iterations
appear in [21]; the Krasnoselskii–Mann iteration [22, 25] underlies our small-anchor schedules, and
its o(1/k) and minimax-optimal refinements [5, 7] together with the stochastic Halpern regularity
theory of [31] frame the schedule-level questions of Section 3. Deterministic parameter-free methods
that exploit hidden contractivity appear very recently in [28] and, for mildly expansive maps, in [15];
we cite these as the deterministic endpoint of Q1 and our Theorem 3.30 provides a self-contained,
explicit (though not rate-optimal on contractions) bridge between the necessity theorem and these
structurally adaptive schemes. We note that in the deterministic (noiseless) setting, trajectory
increment ratios are exact and certify the local modulus on the correct side; the stochastic layer
destroys this certification (Remark 5.13), and, as shown in Remark 3.32, even exact oracle responses
do not certify the global modulus in infinite dimensions: the attaining slope can lie in a direction
that is never probed.
    Stochastic fixed-point iteration. Stochastic quasi-Fejér frameworks [12], stochastic forward–
backward splitting [33], and the stochastic fixed-point theory of [6] provide convergence and error
bounds. The closest work is [8]: stochastic Halpern iteration in normed spaces with mini-batching,
Õ(ε−5 ) oracle complexity for nonexpansive maps, Ω(ε−3 ) lower bound (in ℓ1 -type spaces, resistant-
oracle construction), and O(ε−2 (1 − γ)−3 ) for known-γ contractions. We improve the nonexpansive
exponent to ε−4 on Hilbert spaces and 2-uniformly smooth Banach spaces with a single-point
oracle (Theorem 4.3, via the dualization of Remark 4.4; the Hilbert exponent via the reduction of
Theorem 4.8), and the contraction exponent to (1 − γ)−2 ; Section 5.5 proves their (1 − γ)−3 is tight
for their algorithm (algebraic 1/N bias) but not for the class. Recursive variance reduction achieves
Õ(σ 2 ε−4 ) and, under average-smoothness, Õ(σ 2 ε−3 ) for stochastic monotone inclusions [9], with a
multi-point oracle; under bounded variance, Chen and Luo [11] achieve the tight Θ̃(σ 2 ε−2 + LDε−1 )
for the monotone subclass; our ε−4 uses neither monotonicity nor multi-point access, and neither
does it use the inner product. The parameter-free extragradient line [34] pursues parameter-freeness
for variational inequalities; its guarantees are stated for monotone operators and do not cover
the pure fixed-point residual class of Q3. Very recent work of Diakonikolas develops the gradual
Halpern method GHAL and its parameter-free variant AdaGHAL, which achieve near-optimal
oracle complexity for deterministic Lipschitz fixed-point equations (γ ≤ 1 and mildly expansive) in
general normed and geodesic spaces [15], building on her earlier parameter-free Halpern method
for monotone inclusions [14]; and its stochastic extension VR-GHAL gives anytime high-probability
residual guarantees in quadratically smoothable Banach spaces with bounded-variance oracle
complexity min{ε−5 , (1 − γ)−3 ε−2 }, improving to ε−3 under a Lipschitz-in-expectation oracle and
ε−2 under samplewise nonexpansiveness [16]. Very recent work of Diakonikolas, Guzmán, and
Martı́nez-Rubio [17] develops high-probability oracle-complexity bounds for stochastic fixed-point
equations with nonexpansive maps in arbitrary normed spaces of weak Rademacher type q > 1;
on weak-type-2 spaces — which include all 2-uniformly smooth spaces — their proved bound
Õ(Dσ 2 ε−3 + Dε−1 ) (up to polylogarithms; their Theorem 5) improves on the rate of Theorem 4.3

throughout the regime ε ≤ D/4, and the remaining open exponent question concerns norms that are
not of weak type 2; see Section 7. The comparison with the present paper requires some care: on the
contraction axis our rate Θ(σ 2 ε−2 (1 − γ)−2 ) improves the (1 − γ)-dependence of [16] by a full factor

Remark 5.13). Our high-probability results are: under sub-Gaussian noise (Theorem 5.7) and, via
the replicate–median device, under bounded variance alone (Theorem 5.8); their matching lower
bounds (up to the deterministic term) are proved by the Gaussian likelihood-ratio Lemma 5.6. In
the contractive/Q-learning lane, ℓ∞ analyses give ε−2 -type rates with various (1 − γ)-powers under
monotonicity [36, 10], in the lineage of minimax RL sample complexity [3] and classical stochastic
approximation [32], and Halpern-type anchoring for MDPs is developed in [8, 23]; our affine lower
bound matches the natural statistical floor and shows no monotone-structure assumption is needed
for its validity as a lower bound.
    Lower-bound methodology. Le Cam and Fano two-point arguments for oracle complexity
are standard [35, 27]; our contraction lower bound is a parametric Le Cam argument whose flat
likelihood ratio makes it immune to adaptivity. The high-probability refinement (Lemma 5.6) uses
the exact Gaussian likelihood-ratio geometry instead of a Cramér–Rao bound, which would only
apply to unbiased estimators. The resistant-oracle lower bounds of [8] remain the strongest for the
nonexpansive class.

Positioning against the parameter-free line. The parameter-free schemes [28, 15, 16, 34]
answer the algorithm-design question; this paper answers the complementary complexity questions:
is such adaptivity necessary (yes, exactly when dense anchor scales are non-lacunary; Theorem 3.15),
what is the exact tradeoff of a fixed schedule (a dichotomy with a sharp lacunarity boundary;
Theorems 3.14, 3.33, 3.15), and which modulus information is usable (Theorem 5.19). The cited
works do not address schedule-level separations, dichotomy boundaries, or certification impossibility;
conversely, we do not compete with their algorithmic guarantees, and our Theorems 3.25 and 3.15
are deliberately elementary, addressing different objects.

Remark 2.3 (The measurability discipline). Throughout, stochastic recursions are kept in squared
norms, expanded before any triangle inequality, with coefficients that are deterministic (or copied
from a deterministic comparison path) and bounded pointwise by deterministic envelopes. Two
consequences are used constantly: (i) cross terms ⟨un−1 , ξ¯n ⟩ with un−1 Fn−1 -measurable vanish in
expectation; (ii) no independence between parameters and noise is ever required. Any proof step
violating (i)–(ii) is flagged where it occurs.
    The anchored iteration class is (1) with arbitrary deterministic coefficients (θn ); the span
class of [8] restricts xn+1 to the span of x0 , . . . , xn and the current batch average; all algorithms
of this paper lie in the span class, and all lower bounds are proved against arbitrary adaptive
algorithms unless stated (the noiseless lower bounds of Section 5 are proved against the smaller
anchored/Krasnoselskii–Mann class, Remark 5.11).

1/α0 ≈ 1.71 > 1, not the optimal Θ(D/ε) that the classical schedule meets on Rφ at φ = Θ(1);
and the window minima track the envelope (nφ)−α0 (Remark 3.24, Experiment 5), so on the
evidence of that envelope the schedule is not residual-optimal on N . Whether any single fixed
stream is simultaneously contraction-optimal and residual-optimal on N is open. Two structural
remarks explain the profile. First, rotations are not the distance-hard nonexpansive instances: the
averaged operator 12 (I +Rφ ) has spectral radius cos(φ/2) < 1, so constant-parameter blocks converge
geometrically at rate φ2 /8 — it is anchoring toward x0 , not the rotation, that slows the classical
schedule. Second, the classical blocks do not restore residual-optimality on N : within a block the
weights 1/(n + 2) are ≍ 2−j and nearly constant, so a block damps the residual by at most a constant
factor on worst-case nonexpansive maps. The schedule is genuinely a two-regime (contraction–
rotation) object, and Theorem 3.25 remains the only proved route to the residual-optimal O(D/ε)
on N . Target dependence. The small-mass parameter c′ = ε/(96D) and all stopping horizons in
this paper depend on the target ε and the bound D; this is standard — the classical schedule itself
is only certified up to the horizon N = ⌈4D/ε⌉ — and no schedule here ever uses the modulus.
Consequently the factor-2 overhead of Theorem 3.25 is an artifact of the two-constant-density-track
design, not a barrier : the deterministic necessity question for single-stream schemes is resolved in
the negative — one fixed oscillatory stream, no parallelism, no adaptivity, no overhead, optimal on
contractions and rotation-polynomial (its residual behavior on N is the open part, (c) above). (The
stochastic single-stream analogue of Theorem 5.14 remains open; Remark 5.15.)

of the envelope at the first handoff nφ ≈ 11.6 (bad-set measure ∼ 2 · 10−3 per shell); for each fixed
adversarial angle the dip does not recur at deeper octaves (min-ratio 0.11–0.9 across nφ ∈ [24 , 217 ]),
but each scale has its own adversarial angles and refining the Cantor descent deepens the transition
dips — forcing the universal constant in any pointwise claim toward 0. We therefore assess the

Remark 3.24 (Status of the lacunarity law: what is proved and what is open). We distinguish
four statements. (i) The a.e. hitting-time rate is proved with the exact exponent at each fixed
accuracy (Theorem 3.21(i)): for a.e. φ and every fixed ε, Nφ (ε) ≥ φ−1 µ(φ, ε)ε−1/α(ε) , matching
Theorem 3.15(ii) up to the constant, and by Tonelli the bound holds on a full-density set of accuracies
for a.e. φ; the constant-density case remains fully explicit (Lemma 3.7 and Theorem 3.9(ii)), and the
φ-exponent is proved for every schedule and every angle (Lemma 3.12). (ii) The uniform bound holds
with a non-explicit rate for every β < 1/α0 (Theorem 3.21(ii), proved by Egorov uniformity and the
analyticity of K∞ ); the explicit-rate version would follow from the orbit small-ball (Conjecture 3.20).
Three features of Lemma 3.19 block the single-term Rouché analysis from delivering that small-ball:
the leading chord coefficients are comparable, the top chord frequencies are not separated, and on
deeper blocks the sub-handoff tail is of the same order as the main term; a genuine grouped/lacunary
small-ball for the full orbit transform (of Sidon type, cf. [20, 40]) is the missing ingredient. Reaching
1/α0 uniformly with explicit constants is open. (iii) The pointwise law survives only for Diophantine
angles (Conjecture 3.22) and is likely false universally (Remark 3.23). (iv) The fixed-constant
uniform envelope ∥xn ∥ ≥ c0 (nφ)−α with c0 independent of ε is the statement connected with
locking: its natural proof (the dyadic-chaining argument across scales zj+1 = ρj zj + fj with forcing
|fj | ≤ min{1/3, 2/(nj φ)}) fails at the handoff scale nφ ≍ 1, where worst-case cancellation is vacuous,
and the union bound without locking gives only measure ∼ c0 log(1/ε). We do not know whether the
fixed-envelope statement holds for a.e. φ; it is not needed anywhere in this paper. The mechanism
behind the whole lacunarity analysis is the schedule’s rotation Fourier spectrum: at threshold ε, rare
cancellations of the orbit sum at radius µn (ε) ≍ ε/(rn 2j0 α ) control the hitting time (Lemma 3.19),
and as c → 0 the prefactor Γ(c + 1) → 1 while the exponent −c → 0 quantifies the damping.

3.6    The sufficiency half: optimal escape by parallel composition, and the single-
       trajectory barrier
Theorem 3.9 and Theorem 3.14 prove that no fixed anchor schedule — of the family (2) or with any
density cn ∈ (0, 1] — is simultaneously near-optimal on contractions and polynomial on rotations.

parallel composition of two fixed schedules with stopping at the first success (the classical expert-
tracking device). The result upgrades the adaptivity story from a necessity theorem to a complete
dichotomy.

Remark 3.29 (Analytic hitting times versus certified stopping). Throughout Section 3, hitting times
are the minimax-analytic objects being lower- and upper-bounded, and Theorem 3.25 answers Q1 in
exactly this sense. If instead the scheme must certify from oracle data that its output is ε-accurate,

a residual), but distance accuracy on Cγ requires one-sided modulus information (Remark 5.13),
and with a stochastic oracle no positive sound certificate exists without a certified ceiling, in any
dimension (Theorem 5.19). The parallel scheme is therefore a sharp answer to Q1 as a deterministic
complexity question; its stochastic counterpart must be phrased through the trichotomy of Section 5
— which is precisely what that trichotomy is for.

at all (Remark 3.16).
Remark 3.32 (The detection barrier). The parallel scheme of Theorem 3.25 sidesteps detection
entirely, at factor-2 cost. A single-trajectory scheme must instead decide, from its own trajectory,
when to exploit contractivity; two barriers prevent the naive detector. (a) Hidden directions
(deterministic). Even with exact oracle responses, every probe x 7→ T x reveals only directional
slopes ∥T x − T y∥/∥x − y∥ ≤ γ: an adversary can hide the attaining slope in a direction the trajectory
never probes. Concretely, the construction of Theorem 5.19 works verbatim without noise (σ = 0):
the nominal T0 (x) = γ0 x and the modified T agree on the entire probed set, so no algorithm whose
decisions depend on oracle responses can distinguish them, yet 1 − Lip(T ) = δ1 < κδ1 = κ(1 − LipT0 ).
Hence in infinite dimensions no exact-oracle probing strategy certifies the global modulus, and any
schedule that freezes its window length from a certified modulus guess can be made to freeze at a
wrong rate. (b) Noise. With the stochastic oracle, the natural detector (angle statistics: on Rφ the
increments satisfy ⟨xj − xj−1 , T xj − T xj−1 ⟩ = cos φ ∥xj − xj−1 ∥∥T xj − T xj−1 ∥ exactly, while on a
scalar contraction the angle is 0) must be estimated from Tb-responses with per-evaluation noise σ,
and the estimated angle is correlated with the current noise — precisely the measurability failure
of Remark 2.3. The conclusion: single-trajectory two-regime optimality, if achievable at all, must
be structural (residual-feedback updates that need no certified modulus), as in [28, 15], and the
stochastic version must route through the ceiling trichotomy of Section 5. The stochastic two-regime
problem itself is settled up to the factor 2 by parallel composition (Theorem 5.14); in the deterministic

5     The contraction regime: exact oracle complexity
This section answers Q2. Throughout, Cγ is the class of γ-contractions with ∥x0 − x∗ ∥ ≤ D. The
exact complexity depends on what is known about the modulus γ, and the answer splits into three
regimes: affine instances (no modulus information is needed), general instances with known modulus,
and general instances with only an upper bound on the modulus. That these are the only regimes in
which the distance-certification question is well-posed is the content of Remark 5.13.

Remark 5.13 (The certification barrier). Corollary 5.12 is the correct statement of “parameter-
freeness”: what suffices is a certified upper bound on the modulus. Three observations show that
one cannot do without modulus information in general, and explain why the natural certificate-free
schemes fail.

(a) Residual conversion needs δ from below. For any contraction, ∥x − x∗ ∥ ≤ ∥x − T x∥/δ: a
    residual test at tolerance t certifies distance t/δ, so soundness requires a lower bound on δ,
    equivalently an upper bound on γ.

(b) Trajectory probes bound δ on the wrong side. Every quantity observable from finitely many
    probes is a function of directional moduli ρ = ∥T x − T x′ ∥/∥x − x′ ∥ ≤ γ, and hence of 1 − ρ ≥ δ:
    probes deliver upper bounds on δ, never lower bounds. Consequently, replacing γ by a trajectory
    estimate inside a soundness certificate is invalid for nonlinear maps. A concrete counterexample:
    T (x1 , x2 ) = (1 − 3η)x1 , (1 − η/8)x2 , x0 = (D, 0), with oracle noise supported on the x1 -axis
    (admissible by Definition 2.1). Every probe on the trajectory sees ρ = 1 − 3η, while δ = η/8;
    any scheme whose soundness rests on “the observed gap is at least 2η” fails with probability
    one. Any scheme whose soundness rests on such a one-sided estimate fails in general.

(c) The positive cases. Affine maps are the exception: their modulus is visible exactly (ρ = γ from
    any probe), which is why Theorem 5.3 is γ-free (Corollary 5.4). For general maps, an a priori
    upper bound on γ is precisely the one-sided information soundness needs, and Corollary 5.12
    shows it is also sufficient.


                                                   39
Whether any single algorithm achieves the affine rate simultaneously for all γ on the general class
is open; (a)–(b) rule out all residual-based schemes, and we conjecture the answer is negative
in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, where an alternative instance can hide its distinguishing
structure in directions that are queried with low density. A formal impossibility theorem along these
lines requires a resistant-oracle construction that respects the contraction constraint; we complete it

5.6    Modulus-free certification: the impossibility theorem and the exact bound-
       ary
Remark 5.13 left open whether any sound distance certification is possible without modulus informa-
tion. We now resolve this question completely, in every Hilbert space: sound gap certification from
oracle information alone is impossible unless a certified modulus bound is available (Theorem 5.19);
with a certified ceiling it is possible at the optimal rate (Corollary 5.12), which is therefore the
exact boundary. The construction is the resistant-oracle argument anticipated in Remark 5.13,
made rigorous; the two points that require care are that the hard instance must be fixed before the
interaction (a quantile of the certificate replaces its realized value), and that the hidden bumps
must be missed by the query trajectory with high probability (the locations avoid the atoms of the
query distributions).
    A certificate is any (possibly randomized) function δ̂ = δ̂(transcript) output by the algorithm; it
is sound on an instance T if δ̂ ≤ 1 − Lip(T ) with high probability, since then ∥x̂ − x∗ ∥ ≤ ∥x̂ − T x̂∥/δ̂
follows from ∥x̂ − T x̂∥ ≥ (1 − Lip(T ))∥x̂ − x∗ ∥. Soundness quantified over a class requires the
inequality to hold for every instance consistent with the transcript.

Remark 5.20 (What remains possible). Theorem 5.19 concerns certification, not convergence:
Krasnoselskii–Mann with constant batches drives E∥xn − x∗ ∥2 to the noise floor σ 2 /(mδ) with no
modulus knowledge at all — an algorithm can converge without ever knowing it has converged, and
the theorem says the latter knowledge is exactly what costs modulus information. Together with
Corollaries 5.4, 5.10 and 5.12, the modulus question is now resolved in all regimes: parameter-free
optimal estimation is possible for affine instances; optimal certified solving is possible given any
certified ceiling at cost governed by δ+ ; and without a ceiling, no positive sound certificate exists in
any dimension.

7            Discussion and future research
(i) The Hilbert exponent is settled; on weak-type-2 spaces the upper exponent is
now 3. Theorem 4.8 resolves the Hilbert-space question: the minimax rate is Θ̃(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 ),
exponent 2. For general norms, Theorem 4.3 (valid in every 2-uniformly smooth space with constant
K; see Remark 4.4) improved on the then-best bound Õ(σ 2 ε−5 ) [8] by reaching O(Kσ 2 D2 ε−4 ).
While this paper was being finalized, Diakonikolas, Guzmán and Martı́nez-Rubio [17] proved, for
the same single-point bounded-variance oracle and the same residual criterion, a high-probability
algorithm with complexity Õ(Dσ 2 ε−3 + Dε−1 ) on every space of weak Rademacher type 2 (their
Theorem 5 with q = 2); the diameter enters linearly, up to polylogarithms, and the class contains all
2-uniformly smooth Banach spaces — precisely the range in which Theorem 4.3 was advertised as an


                                                                   50
improvement. The ratio of the two bounds is ε/(KD), so theirs is the better rate whenever ε ≲ KD,
and in particular over the whole regime ε ≤ D/4 treated here. Theorem 4.3 keeps a different role: it
is a short, single-loop argument that uses only anchoring and batching, and the same computation
also produces the noise floor behind Section 5. The exponent record on type-2 spaces, however,
now belongs to [17]. What remains open is the exponent itself for norms that are not of weak type
2 — the ℓ1 -type cases: there the best upper bound is still Õ(σ 2 ε−5 ) [8], against the lower bound
Ω(Dε−1 + Dσ 2 ε−3 ) proved in [17] against arbitrary randomized algorithms. Two remarks on the
Hilbert case may still be worth recording. First, plain anchored iteration as in (1) is not rate-optimal
even on averaged operators: its deviation recursion is controlled only by nonexpansiveness, giving
a stationary floor σ 2 /(mθ) with no dependence on the averaging parameter, which is why its
cost on the rotation class is Θ(1/(φε)) iterations (Lemma 3.6; for c = 1 the worst phase of the
oscillation governs the hitting time), while RAIN solves the same instances in Õ(σ 2 ε−2 + Dε−1 )
independently of φ. Second, the mean-squared-smoothness framework of [2] does not apply to our
class (nonexpansiveness of I − α∇f forces convexity, which excludes their nonconvex constructions),
so the tightness of the σ 2 ε−2 stochastic term for the pure fixed-point residual class (as opposed to
the inclusion class) is inherited from [11] rather than proved directly here; the underlying convex
gradient-norm rate Θ̃(σ 2 ε−2 ) is due to [18, 1].
    (ii) Resolved: geometric batching removes the logarithm; the modulus question is
resolved exactly. Theorem 5.5 refutes the natural conjecture that anchored schemes with constant
batches require the logarithmic factor. The exact affine complexity is Θ(σ 2 ε−2 δ −2 ) (Corollary 5.4)
with no logarithm; and the general (nonlinear) complexity, for known γ, is the same up to the
deterministic term (Corollary 5.10) — there is no linear/nonlinear dichotomy. The mechanism:
constant-batch anchored or Krasnoselskii–Mann schemes converge to their noise floor σ 2 /(mδ) in
Θ(ln(D/ε)/δ) steps, giving Õ(σ 2 εP−2 δ −2 ln(D/ε)), but the optimal control of the variance recursion

vk+1 ≤ γ 2 vk + σ 2 /mk (minimize k mk subject to vT ≤ ε2 ) has the constant-Hamiltonian solution
mk ∝ γ T −k : noise injected early is attenuated by γ 2(T −k) by the end, so early steps tolerate small
batches and the total is O(σ 2 /(δ 2 ε2 )). Three natural shortcuts fail, and we record them in one
line each: multiscale batching mj ≍ σ 2 /(δ 2 rj2 ) costs Ω(σ 2 ε−2 δ −3 ); no constant-θ anchored schedule
removes the geometric phase (the anchor bias forces θ ≲ εδ/D); and the natural lower-bound
constructions fail against Theorem 5.5 (known-Qv families are directly invertible, and shell-gated
slope families are defeated by geometric batching, which harvests per-shell information along the
trajectory for free). The unknown-modulus question itself is resolved in Section 5.6 and only restated
here: sound data-driven certification without a certified ceiling is impossible in every dimension
(Theorem 5.19), and the ceiling is the exact boundary (Corollary 5.12).
    (iii) Single-trajectory and stochastic instance-adaptivity. The deterministic adaptivity
question is settled outright: the oscillatory schedule (Theorem 3.15) is a single fixed stream, optimal
on contractions and rotation-polynomial with the lacunarity exponent 1/α0 (Theorem 3.15(ii)), with
no parallelism, no adaptivity, and no factor-2 overhead; the parallel two-track scheme (Theorem 3.25)
remains the simplest witness, and Remark 3.32 shows that statistic-based detection on a single
trajectory cannot reach the optimum. (The doubling-window scheme (Theorem 3.30) improves
the classical tradeoff only by a constant factor, Corollary 3.27 shows the dichotomy is a single-
instance phenomenon, and the three obstructions of Remark 3.28 explain why statistic-based
detection cannot do what oscillation does.) What remains open: (a) the stochastic single-stream
analogue of Theorem 5.14 (one evaluation per step carrying both guarantees, with the contraction
guarantee at the optimal δ+ -rate; the master noise lemma provides the envelope machinery, the
certification barrier (Theorem 5.19) forbids data-driven distance stopping rules, and the measurability
discipline (Remark 2.3) forbids trajectory-estimated coefficients, so a positive answer would require a
structurally adaptive single-stream scheme); (b) the pointwise lacunarity law for Diophantine angles

                                                    51
(Conjecture 3.22; for a.e. angle the rate is exact with exponent 1/α0 (Theorem 3.21(i)), the universal
pointwise statement is likely false (Remark 3.23), and uniform bad sets have measure o(1) for every
β < 1/α0 (Theorem 3.21(ii)) with explicit rates open via the orbit small-ball (Conjecture 3.20)); (c)
the extension of the lacunarity analysis to non-block lacunary patterns; (d) whether any single fixed
stream is simultaneously contraction-optimal and residual-optimal on N (the oscillatory schedule
is not, up to the envelope evidence of Remark 3.16(c)); (e) stochastic instance-adaptivity for
nonexpansive maps — an algorithm whose complexity on error-bounded nonexpansive instances
improves over the worst-case ε−4 without a modulus, in the spirit of Theorem 3.30.
    (iv) State-dependent variance and heavy tails. All upper bounds assume uniformly
bounded variance; the state-dependent model E∥ξ∥2 ≤ σ 2 (1 + ∥x∥2 ) is treatable by a discrete
Gronwall step at the cost of logarithmic factors, and heavy-tailed oracles require truncation
arguments that we have not pursued.
    (v) High-probability uniformity and anytime variants. Proposition 4.5 gives fixed-horizon
confidence; uniform-in-n guarantees and ε-free (anytime) versions of Theorem 5.5 via the doubling
trick are standard refinements. The one unresolved point is the factor ln(D/ε) separating the upper
and lower bounds in the stochastic term of Theorem 5.8 for bounded-variance noise; we do not know
whether replicate–median is lossy here or whether a matching lower bound with the extra logarithm
holds.
