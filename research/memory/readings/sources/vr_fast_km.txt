                                                We develop a new class of fast Krasnosel’skiǐ-Mann methods with variance reduction to solve a finite-sum
                                               cocoercive equation Gx = 0. Our algorithm is single-loop and leverages a new family of unbiased variance-
                                               reduced estimators specifically designed for a wider class of root-finding algorithms. Our method achieves
                                               both O(1/k2 ) and o(1/k2 ) last-iterate convergence rates in terms of E[∥Gxk ∥2 ], where k is the iteration
                                               counter and E[·] is the total expectation. We also establish almost-sure o(1/k2 ) convergence rates and the
                                               almost-sure convergence of iterates {xk } to a solution of Gx = 0. We instantiate our framework for two
                                               prominent estimators: SVRG and     SAGA. By an appropriate choice of parameters, both variants attain an
                                               oracle complexity of O n + n2/3 ϵ−1 to reach an ϵ-solution, where n denotes the number of summands in
                                               the finite-sum operator G. Furthermore, under σ-strong quasi-monotonicity,       our method achieves a linear
                                               convergence rate and an oracle complexity of O n + max{n, n2/3 κ} log( 1ϵ ) , where κ := L/σ. We extend our
                                                                                                                            
                                               approach to solve a class of finite-sum inclusions (possibly nonmonotone), demonstrating that our schemes
                                               retain the same theoretical guarantees as in the equation setting. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrate
                                               that our methods exhibit promising performance compared with state-of-the-art methods.
                                               Key words : Variance reduction method; fast Krasnosel’skiǐ-Mann method; Nesterov’s accelerated method;
                                                 finite-sum cocoercive equation; finite-sum inclusion.

Comparison with prior works. We now briefly compare our contributions with existing works
to highlight key differences.
 • First, our unbiased variance-reduced estimator Sek , including SVRG and SAGA, is specifically
    designed for S k , not for Gxk . This differs from existing works such as [2, 3, 12, 14, 30, 33, 39,
    49]. Note that [2, 3, 14, 30] only consider specific SVRG and/or SAGA estimators to directly
    approximate Gxk , while [12, 33, 39, 49] cover a wide class of estimators, possibly including
    SVRG and SAGA as special instances. In Subsection 2.1 below, we will further compare our
    class of estimators with these related works.
 • Second, our method (VFKM) is single-loop and uses simple operations, making it easier to
    implement than double-loop or catalyst-based algorithms in [48, 81].
 • Third, our convergence rates and oracle complexity estimates rely on the metric E ∥Gxk ∥2 or
                                                                                                   

    ∥Gxk ∥2 , which can be viewed as a type of Fukushima’s regularized gap function [36] for (CE).
    This metric differs from recent results, e.g., [2, 3], that use a Nesterov’s gap or restricted gap

 • Fourth, our method achieves an O (1/k)-factor improvement over non-accelerated methods in

    or SVRG, without requiring enhancements such as restarting or nested strategies as in, e.g.,
    [4, 86]. This complexity is better than deterministic accelerated methods by a factor n1/3 .

   1.5. Related work. Both (CE) and (NI) are well-studied in the literature (e.g., [10, 16, 34,
70, 73, 74]). We focus on the most recent works relevant to our methods.
   Accelerated methods. Nesterov’s accelerated methods have been applied to solve both (CE) and
(NI) in [42, 51] and [8], followed by [1, 5, 6, 8, 24, 42, 50, 60, 67, 77]. Unlike convex optimization,
extending these methods to (CE) and (NI) faces a fundamental challenge in constructing a suitable
Lyapunov function. In convex optimization, the objective function serves this purpose, but it is
absent in (CE) and (NI). This necessitates a different approach for (CE) and (NI) (see, e.g., [8, 60]).
Our approach leverages similar ideas from dynamical systems as in [8, 60].
   Alternatively, Halpern’s fixed-point iteration [41] has recently been proven to achieve better
convergence rates (see [32, 57, 75]), matching Nesterov’s schemes. Starting with the seminal work
[82], which extended Halpern’s iteration to the extragradient method, many subsequent works have
exploited this idea for other methods (e.g., [19, 20, 56, 67, 76, 77, 78]). Recently, [77] established a
connection between Nesterov’s and Halpern’s accelerations for solving (CE) with different schemes.
Nevertheless, all of these results are primarily developed for deterministic methods.
   Stochastic approximation methods. Stochastic methods for both (CE) and (NI) and their special
cases have been extensively studied, e.g., in [46, 52, 69]. Some methods exploit mirror-prox and
averaging techniques such as [46, 52], while others rely on projection or extragradient-type schemes
(e.g., [13, 27, 44, 47, 62, 69, 83]). Many algorithms use standard Robbins-Monro stochastic approx-
imation with large or increasing batch sizes to reduce the variance. Other works generalize the
analysis to a broader class of algorithms (e.g., [12, 38, 58]), covering both standard stochastic and
variance reduction methods. However, their complexity is generally worse than that of methods
based on control variate techniques as in our work.
   Variance reduction methods. Control variate variance reduction techniques are widely used in
optimization, leading to the development of various estimators, including SAGA [31], SVRG [45],
SARAH [65], and Hybrid-SGD [79]. These estimators have been adopted in methods for solving
(CE) and (NI). For instance, [29, 30] proposed a SAGA-type method for (CE) and (NI), under
“star” cocoercivity and strong quasi-monotonicity — assumptions closely related to our work.
Nevertheless, our focus is on accelerated methods that achieve improved convergence rates and
complexity. Similarly, [2, 3] employed SVRG estimators for methods related to (NI), but these
algorithms are non-accelerated. Other relevant works include [14, 21, 23, 43, 66, 84], some of which
focused on minimax problems or bilinear matrix games. More recently, variance-reduced methods
based on Halpern’s fixed-point iteration have been explored (e.g., [17, 18]), leveraging SARAH to
achieve improved complexity. While this approach offers advantages in certain settings, our work
takes a different path by focusing on Nesterov’s acceleration combined with variance reduction
techniques. In addition, we prove new and stronger convergence results compared to these papers.
   1.6. Notation. We work with a finite-dimensional space Rp equipped with the standard inner
product ⟨·, ·⟩ and norm ∥ · ∥. For a single-valued or multivalued operator F on Rp , dom(F ) := {x ∈
Rp : F x ̸= ∅} denotes its domain, gra(F ) := {(x, z) ∈ dom(F ) × Rp : z ∈ F x} denotes its graph, and
JF x := {u ∈ dom(F ) : x ∈ u + F u} denotes its resolvent. For a symmetric matrix X, λmin (X) and
λmax (X) stand for the smallest and largest eigenvalues of X, respectively.
   We use Fk to denote the σ-algebra generated by all the randomness arising from the algorithm,
including x0 , · · · , xk , up to the iteration k before constructing Sek . Ek [·] = E[· | Fk ] denotes the
conditional expectation with respect to Fk , and E[·] is the total expectation. We also use O (·),
o (·), and Ω(·) to characterize convergence rates and oracle complexity as usual.

Discussion. Let us further discuss Definition 1 and compare it with related existing works.
 1. The first condition in (6) shows that Sek is an unbiased estimator of S k . The second line of (6)
    provides an upper bound on the variance of Sek , which depends on both the parameter γk in S k
    and the random variable ∆k . The last condition in (6) establishes a “quasi-Fejér property” for
    (1 − γk )−2 ∆k with contraction factor 1 − ρ and two vanishing terms Uk and Uk−1 . This condition
    involves evaluations of Gi at three consecutive iterates xk−2 , xk−1 , and xk .
 2. Since Definition 1 defines a class of estimators Sek for S k = Gxk − γk Gxk−1 , rather than for
    Gxk alone, it differs from the definitions in [12, 33, 39, 49], which are formulated for Gxk
    or ∇f (xk ). More specifically, both [39] and [49] use the same definition of variance-reduced
    unbiased estimators. While their definition has a similar pattern to (6), it involves quantities
    different from S k and Uk in Definition 1. In fact, our Definition 1 does not reduce to a special
    case of those in [39] and [49] when applied to finite-sum convex optimization. Along similar
    lines, [33] introduced a class of estimators that covers both unbiased and biased estimators
    to develop accelerated methods. Although it shares a similar pattern with (6), the quantities
    involved also differ when specialized to convex optimization. The authors in [12] adopted the
    definition in [39, 49] and extended it to VIPs, which is not the same as (6) since it measures
    ∥Ge k − Gx⋆ ∥2 (where G e k is an estimator of Gxk ) instead of ∥Sek − S k ∥2 and uses different terms
    on the right-hand sides of the corresponding inequalities.

   4. Linear Convergence under Strong Quasi-monotonicity. In this section, we establish
a linear convergence rate and estimate the oracle complexity of (VFKM) under Assumption 1 and
the following additional assumption:
  Assumption 2. G in (CE) is σ-strongly quasi-monotone, i.e., there exist x⋆ ∈ zer(G) and σ > 0

If G is strongly monotone with a strong monotonicity modulus σ > 0, then it satisfies Assumption 2.
However, the converse is not true in general. For example, if we consider G : R → R such that

than the strong monotonicity of G. Note that we do not require each summand Gi to be strongly
monotone. Assumption 2 is not new. It has been used in [68] and recently in [30, 58].
