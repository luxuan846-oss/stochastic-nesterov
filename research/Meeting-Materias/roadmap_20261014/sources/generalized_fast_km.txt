     We study accelerated Krasnoselskii–Mann-type methods with preconditioners in both continuous and
     discrete time. From a continuous-time model we derive a generalized fast Krasnoselskii–Mann method,
     providing a new yet simple proof of convergence that leads to unprecedented flexibility in parameter
     tuning, allowing up to twice larger relaxation parameters. Our analysis unifies inertial and anchoring
     acceleration mechanisms and offers a broad range of parameter choices, which prove beneficial in practice.
     Additionally, we extend our analysis to the case where preconditioners are allowed to be degenerate,
     unifying the treatment of various splitting methods and establishing the weak convergence of shadow
     sequences.

     Keywords: inertial accelerations; Nesterov acceleration; Halpern method; fast Krasnosel’ki˘ı–Mann
     method; degenerate proximal point algorithm.



1. Introduction
Let T : H → H be a nonexpansive operator on the real Hilbert space H . Our objective is to solve the
fixed-point problem:

                                                   Find x ∈ H such that x = T (x),                                                           (1.1)

with fast methods in continuous and discrete time, and apply these results to splitting methods providing
convergence of the so-called shadow sequences (to be made precise later).
    If T = I − ∇ f , where f : H → R is a convex and smooth function whose set of minimizers is
nonempty, (1.1) is equivalent to minimizing f . The most popular accelerated method to minimize f in
discrete time is the celebrated Nesterov’s algorithm (Nesterov, 1983). Su et al. (2016) later discovered
that Nesterov’s algorithm can be understood as a specific finite differences discretization of the following
ODE:

For instance, to solve (1.1), it is natural to consider Q := I − T , which is indeed monotone and Lipschitz,
and the equilibria of (1.4) are solutions to (1.1). Note that while oscillations attenuation with Hessian-
driven damping are quite well understood and intuitive in optimization contexts (where Q = ∇ f ),
the interpretation of the geometric damping for general monotone operators is not always clear and
remains an open problem. In fact, the geometric damping might not always reduce oscillations for general
monotone operators as we will see in some case in our numerical results. Nonetheless, we will still stick
with this terminology in this paper for consistency reasons.
    Although the system in (1.3) generates fast converging trajectories for a range of admissible
parameters (cf. (Attouch et al., 2023, theorem 2.1)), when one turns to the general operator setting (1.4)
the situation becomes significantly more delicate. A notable contribution in this direction was made by
Boţ et al. (2025b), who studied (1.4) with b(t) := 12 (β˙ (t) + αt β (t)), the so-called fast Optimistic Gradient
Descent Ascent (Fast-OGDA) system. In particular, they showed that when β is constant and α > 2 this
dynamical system generates trajectories weakly converging to a zero of Q (provided that the set of zeros
of Q is nonempty) with a convergence rate of Q(x(t)) = o(t−1 ) as t → +∞. Therefore, (1.4) can be
seen as an accelerated version of the first-order system

connection between Halpern’s method and momentum-type algorithms have been observed previously
in Contreras & Cominetti (2023); Tran-Dinh (2024); Yoon et al. (2024).

algorithmic framework to design accelerated variants of operator splitting methods, such as primal-dual
(Chambolle & Pock, 2011), forward–backward (Beck & Teboulle, 2009), Douglas–Rachford (Douglas
& Rachford, 1956; Lions & Mercier, 1979), Davis–Yin (Raguet et al., 2013; Davis & Yin, 2017) and
several other extensions. Following the recent insights in He & Yuan (2012); Bredies et al. (2022,

in a renormed Hilbert space, see Section 4. In this context the metric might actually be degenerate,
i.e., only inducing a Hilbert seminorm, and thus the analysis requires a careful adaptation. We do so by
delineating a degenerate Opial Lemma, a fundamental ingredient that allows us to adapt the convergence
analysis of Algorithm 1 almost effortlessly to this degenerate setting. We apply this result to accelerate
the convergence of a recent extension of the Douglas–Rachford splitting method (Lions & Mercier, 1979;
Bredies et al., 2024), ensuring the weak convergence of the so-called shadow sequences in an infinite-
dimensional Hilbert space setting.

REMARK 2 (On the growth of β ). The parameter function β can be understood as a time-scaling parameter
(Attouch et al., 2019b). As such, it directly impacts the rate of convergence, as shown in Theorem
1(iii). However, contrarily to classical autonomous first-order dynamical systems, where the time-scaling
parameter can grow arbitrarily fast while maintaining convergence, the nonautonomous nature of our
second-order dynamical system—particularly the fixed decay rate of the vanishing damping parameter
α
 t —imposes a fundamental constraint on the growth of β . Indeed, applying Grönwall’s inequality to
integrate (2.6), we get that β (t) must satisfy

                                                     β (t) ≤ β0 tα −2

for β0 > 0. Clearly, the growth of β (t) depends on the vanishing damping parameter α . Choosing
β (t) = tα −2−ε with ε ∈ (0, α −2), we can achieve arbitrarily fast decay rates o(t−ν ), where ν := α −1−ε,
by increasing α .

REMARK 6 (A crucial performance trade-off). When α = σ = 2 Algorithm 1 specializes to OHM,
which is known to achieve optimal worst-case fixed-point residual decay among the class of first-order
methods—in a sense very close in spirit to Nemirovsky–Yudin result for convex optimization. More
precisely, in Diakonikolas & Wang (2022); Park & Ryu (2022) it is stated that for any dimension n there
exists an operator on Rn such that no other method in this class yields a smaller fixed-point residual
at iteration n. However, as shown in Contreras & Cominetti (2023) in the cased of Banach spaces this
optimality in worst-case rates comes with a twist: OHM may not satisfy a little-o decay, meaning that
there exist instances where the fixed-point residual is uniformly upper bounded by Ck for all k ∈ N, for
some constant C > 0. Our numerical experiments in Section 5 suggest that this phenomenon is not just
theoretical—it typically occurs in practice. On the other hand, choosing parameters that allow Algorithm
1 to depart from OHM (e.g., α > 2) may sacrifice worst-case optimality, but enables little-o convergence
rates. This guarantees that for each instance there exists a kˆ beyond which the residual is strictly lower
than Ck . While kˆ may be large and the acceleration effect delayed, we observe that in many practical
scenarios the convergence is significantly faster.

6. Conclusion
In this paper we introduced a generalized version of the Fast-KM method proposed in Boţ & Nguyen
(2023), incorporating two additional degrees of flexibility through the parameters θ ∈ (1, α − 1)
and σ > 0. We provided a new convergence proof based on a semi-implicit discretization of a
continuous-time model. Notably, our approach encompasses the case α = 2, which corresponds to
the Optimal Halpern Method, thereby unifying the convergence analysis of these two acceleration

mechanisms. We demonstrated how these results can be applied to operator splitting methods potentially
with preconditioners, offering critical convergence guarantees for the so-called shadow sequences.
Furthermore, our numerical experiments showcased the benefits afforded by the added flexibility of
the method. This paper opens up new intriguing questions: (i) what is the tight nonasymptotic rate for
